## KQ1. TMR efficacy: meta-analyses, effect sizes, moderators, and whether REM-TMR helps procedural / emotional / creative memory

### Takeaway
The only peer-reviewed quantitative synthesis remains Hu et al. (2020): sleep TMR has a small-to-moderate effect (Hedges' g = 0.29) that is significant only for N2 and SWS cueing, with REM cueing non-significant (g = −0.06). No peer-reviewed 2022–2026 meta-analysis was found; the 2024 narrative update (Carbone & Diekelmann) and 2021–2025 primary REM-TMR studies suggest REM cueing can act on emotional tone, rule abstraction and whole-body procedural learning, often with delayed or weak behavioral effects, while several recent studies report nulls or even impairment.

### Cited Findings
- Hu, Cheng, Chiu & Paller (2020), Psychological Bulletin 146:218–244: 91 experiments, 212 effect sizes, N = 2,004; overall sleep TMR Hedges' g = 0.29 [0.21, 0.38]; N2 g = 0.32 [0.04, 0.60] (k = 13); SWS g = 0.27 [0.20, 0.35] (k = 174); REM g = −0.06 [−0.31, 0.18] (7 experiments, 142 participants, k = 15, p = .616); "unspecified"/not EEG-supervised cueing g = 0.26 ns; wake g = 0.07 ns; cueing stage moderator Q(4) = 10.74, p = .03 — [Hu et al. 2020 PDF](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/paller/PsychBulletin2020.pdf); [PMC7144680](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680)
- Hu et al. (2020): auditory non-verbal, auditory verbal and olfactory cues gave comparable effects (Q(2) = 0.68, p = .709; verbal g ≈ 0.23 vs non-verbal g ≈ 0.26); between- and within-subject designs were equivalent (Q(1) = 0.055); only 72 of 212 sleep-TMR effect sizes were individually significant, so the authors recommend designing studies "with relatively high statistical power"; TMR did not reliably alter emotional memories or induce fear extinction (mixed evidence) — [Hu et al. 2020 PDF](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/paller/PsychBulletin2020.pdf)
- Lieber (bioRxiv, posted 2019, never found in a journal; PP): 41 TMR recall studies identified, 26 pooled; "small to medium" effect on recall, independent of cue modality, dependent on memory type; methodological quality rated "good" — [bioRxiv 10.1101/796458](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/796458)
- Carbone & Diekelmann (2024), npj Science of Learning 9 (PR narrative review): REM TMR "has rarely been investigated"; summarizes Hutchison 2021 (REM cueing reduced arousal to negative pictures), Yuksel (emotional items enhanced by SWS cues, impaired by REM cues), Pereira/Abdellahi (REM cueing improved rule abstraction only at 1-week follow-up), Picard-Deland 2021 (VR flying task benefited most from REM TMR; dreams about the task predicted gains) and Picard-Deland & Nielsen 2022 (REM TMR increased task dreams 1–2 days later, SWS TMR 5–6 days later). The authors conclude "the timing of cue presentation in relation to the ongoing SO and spindle activity is critical for TMR effects", note several recent null results (Wick & Rasch 2023; Nicolas et al. 2023 in older adults), and call for larger samples, multi-lab replications and preregistration — [PMC11018807](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018807/)
- Cordi & Rasch (2021), Current Opinion in Neurobiology 67:1–7: several key sleep–memory findings "could not be replicated or occurred only under certain conditions"; effects are "smaller, more task-dependent, less SWS-related, less robust and less long-lasting than previously assumed"; they call for replications, meta-analyses and higher reproducibility standards — [doi:10.1016/j.conb.2020.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conb.2020.06.002)
- Moderators of NREM TMR summarized by Carbone & Diekelmann 2024: SO up-state cueing beats down-state (Ngo & Staresina 2022) but Göldi 2019 and Wang 2022 found no up/down difference; a ~3–6 s spindle refractory period after which cues work better (Antony 2018); benefit independent of 1, 2 or 6 items sharing a cue (Schechtman 2021); stronger pre-sleep memories gained more (Ziqing 2024); cues that caused arousals weakened memories (Whitmore & Paller 2023); lower sound intensity and SWS targeting helped in the home SleepStim study (Whitmore 2022); home benefits appeared only on the 3rd night (Göldi & Rasch 2019) — [PMC11018807](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018807/)
- Schechtman et al. (2021), Communications Biology 4:25: cueing object locations grouped 1, 2 or 6 per sound; multiple memories were reactivated "as effectively as a single memory" — [doi:10.1038/s42003-020-01512-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-020-01512-0)
- Ngo & Staresina (2022), PNAS 119(44): N = 24; closed-loop (OpenViBE, Fz) cues at SO UP state (> +35 µV local max) vs DOWN state (< −75 µV local min), 500-ms sounds, 8-s pause after each; UP cueing ≈5 % forgetting vs DOWN ≈15 % (t(23) = 2.28, p = .032); uncued ≈12 % not different from either; ~78 cues per condition; volume titrated from 20 dB SPL in +6 dB steps "until a clear EEG response appeared without signs of arousal" — [PMC9636934](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9636934)
- Nicolas et al. (2025), Nature Communications: closed-loop TMR at SO up- vs down-state for motor sequence learning; up-state cueing gave greater performance improvement, higher SO amplitude and peak-nested sigma, and strengthened striato-motor/hippocampal network activity — [PMC11920436](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11920436/)
- Rakowska et al. (2021), NeuroImage: bimanual SRTT; TMR benefit significant at 10 days but not at 24 h or 6 weeks; N2 time (not N3) predicted benefit; spindle density and SO–spindle coupling rose during cue periods — [PMC8591408](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8591408/)
- REM TMR (procedural): Abdellahi, Koopman, Treder & Lewis (2023), eLife 12:e84324: N = 14 analyzed; SRTT tones replayed in stable REM (1.5 s between tones, 20-s break per sequence, paused on any arousal or REM exit); wake-trained classifier detected reactivation peaking ~1 s after cue (above adaptation night p = 0.004); classifier strength correlated with cued-sequence improvement r = 0.56, p = 0.04; but REM TMR produced no behavioral benefit on its own (cued vs uncued p = 0.43) — [eLife 84324](https://elifesciences.org/articles/84324)
- REM TMR (abstraction): Pereira, Santamaria, Andrews, Schmidt, Van Rossum & Lewis (2023), J Neurosci 43(21):3838–3848: N = 27 within-subject (SWS and REM cued in same night); 8 sounds per stage, 200 ms, 4-s spacing, 14 loops ≈ 15 min, 112 cue events per condition, brown-noise background, cueing stopped at any arousal/stage change; REM-cued problems improved only at the 1-week retest (t(26) = 3.36, p = .002, d = 0.646; day-1 p = .55); SWS cueing no effect; stage × cueing F(1,26) = 6.09, p = .020; REM cueing was continuous in only 1 of 26 participants — [PMC10218979](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10218979/)
- REM TMR (whole-body procedural + dreams): Picard-Deland, Aumont, Samson-Richer, Paquette & Nielsen (2021), Neurobiology of Learning and Memory: VR flying task; tones replayed in SWS, REM, wake or not at all during a morning nap; learning benefited most from REM TMR vs control-sleep; REM dreams reactivating kinesthetic task elements predicted larger gains; TMR itself did not change dream content but its effect was larger when task-dreams co-occurred — [doi:10.1016/j.nlm.2021.107460](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2021.107460)
- Picard-Deland & Nielsen (2022), Journal of Sleep Research: TMR had a delayed, stage-specific effect on dream content — more task dreams 1–2 days later after REM cueing and 5–6 days later after SWS cueing (n = 65 nap, 32 read) — [doi:10.1111/jsr.13391](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13391)
- REM TMR (emotional): Hutchison et al. (2021), Communications Biology: 15 REM / 18 SWS women analyzed; 3-s sounds ×5 each, 3-s gaps, mixed into ~40 dB SPL brown noise; TMR paused on movement, stage change or visible alpha; REM cueing reduced subjective arousal (F(1,14) = 7.48, p < .02), driven by negative items (t = 3.21, p = .006); SWS cueing no effect; no memory effect — [PMC7994443](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7994443/)
- Yuksel et al. (2025), Communications Biology 8 (preprint title: "Emotional memories are enhanced when reactivated in SWS but impaired in REM"): daytime-nap TMR; "reactivating emotional stimuli during REM impairs memory"; SWS benefit correlated with REM × SWS time; emotional items benefited more than neutral — [PMC11930935](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11930935/)
- Greco et al. (2025), Imaging Neuroscience: N = 18; 24 negative picture–sound pairs, each cued 20× (480 cues, 400 ms, 2–4 s jitter) in overnight REM only; volume set individually "so participants could hear the cues without waking", lowered on any arousal sign, paused outside stable REM; no main effect on subjective arousal (p = .45) but cueing × baseline interaction (p = .010; highly arousing items rated lower); reduced dACC, anterior insula and lateral OFC responses and smaller heart-rate deceleration for cued items; no memory measure, no control sounds — [PMC12516159](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12516159/)
- Sato et al. (2025), SLEEP Advances (pilot, N = 16): odor TMR in REM increased the late positive potential to previously seen negative images but did not change memory, arousal or valence — [PMC12199615](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12199615/)
- Sterpenich et al. (2021), Nature Communications: fMRI decoding showed spontaneous (uncued) reactivation of rewarded task patterns during SWS; this is not a REM-cueing study (contrary to how it is sometimes cited) — [PMC8260738](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8260738/)
- Göldi & Rasch (2019), npj Science of Learning (home baseline): N = 66, 3 nights of unsupervised mp3 Dutch-word TMR; benefit only in participants reporting no disturbance; words impaired memory in those reporting word-specific disturbance; whole-sample benefit only on night 3 — [PMC6497651](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6497651/)

### Inferences
- For a home REM-gated system, the honest evidence base is: NREM/SWS TMR = small, reasonably robust effect; REM TMR = plausible for emotional re-tuning, abstraction and whole-body skills, but behavioral effects are small, delayed (1 week in Pereira 2023; 10 days in Rakowska 2021 for NREM) and sometimes negative (Yuksel 2025). Outcome schedules should therefore include a ~7–10-day delayed retest, not only next-morning testing.
- Because Hu 2020 found no benefit when cueing was not EEG-supervised ("unspecified" stage), stage-locked gating with EEG is itself an evidence-based differentiator vs timer-based apps.
- Cue counts in REM studies cluster around 100–500 cues per night with 2–4 s or 10–30 s spacing and strict pause-on-arousal rules; those parameters are a reasonable default envelope.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed meta-analysis of TMR published 2022–2026 was found; the Lieber bioRxiv preprint is from 2019 and unreviewed. A targeted PubMed/PsycINFO check for 2025–2026 meta-analyses (e.g., on REM-TMR or emotional TMR) was not possible after the search budget was exhausted.
- Learning-strength and cue-count moderators in REM TMR specifically are not quantified anywhere found.

## KQ2. Puzzle / problem-solving TMR (Sanders 2019 and follow-ups)

### Takeaway
Sanders et al. (2019) remains the key positive result (31.7 % vs 20.5 % cued vs uncued puzzles solved, dz = 0.47, N = 57, SWS cueing at home with a Zeo headband). A 2021 lab replication with a different (single video-game) problem found a strong sleep effect but no TMR gain in SWS or REM, and the 2026 Paller-lab REM/TLR puzzle study found no overall cueing effect (30 % vs 22 %) but a benefit restricted to participants whose dreams incorporated the cued puzzles.

### Cited Findings
- Sanders, Osburn, Paller & Beeman (2019), Psychological Science 30(11):1616–1624: 61 enrolled, 57 analyzed; pool of 42 puzzles (rebus, matchstick, spatial, verbal), each paired with a unique non-verbal 15-s sound (8–12 s sound + 3–7 s silence); 12 unsolved puzzles over two evenings, 2-min attempts with the sound looping; half of unsolved puzzles cued at home via Zeo wireless EEG (30-s staging); cue played 3 min when SWS detected ≥1.5 min in a 2-min window, pink noise from sleep onset, volume only "low enough" to avoid waking; morning: sound–puzzle recall (71.9 %), then single 4-min solving attempt per puzzle; cued 31.7 % vs uncued 20.5 % solved, t(56) = 3.22, p = .002, dz = 0.47 [0.14, 0.79]; cued-puzzle memory correlated with cued solving r = .37, p = .004 (uncorrected); not preregistered — [PMC6843748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6843748)
- Beijamini, Valentin, Jäger, Born & Diekelmann (2021), Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience 15:645110: Exp 1 sleep vs wake on a video-game insight problem — 61.9 % vs 23.8 % solved (χ² = 6.22, p = .013, φ = 0.39), solvers had more SWS (96.9 vs 67.1 min, d = 1.19); Exp 2 TMR in SWS (n = 18), REM (n = 19) or wake (n = 21) — 55.6 %, 57.9 %, 57.1 % solved (p = .99): "no additional gain through TMR"; authors note REM cues started ~90 min later than SWS cues and that game-derived sounds may have reinforced old strategies — [PMC7965947](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7965947/)
- Konkoly, Morris, Hurka, Martinez, Sanders & Paller (2026), Neuroscience of Consciousness niaf067 (published 5 Feb 2026): N = 20 (mostly frequent lucid dreamers), 35 overnight PSG sessions; ~5.4 puzzles attempted per evening, 4 kept unsolved, 2 cued in REM and 2 not; each puzzle paired with a 15-s soundtrack; TLR used to induce lucidity; cues every 15–30 s from the lowest detectable volume, increased only while soundly asleep, paused on EMG rise, alpha, or loss of REM eye movements/theta; per REM period ≈9.5 music cues, 2.0 title cues, 13.3 lucidity cues; cued puzzles incorporated more into dreams (β = 1.84, p < .001; 7.8 % in non-lucid vs 24.6 % in lucid dreams); conceptual incorporation predicted next-morning solving (β = 1.24, p = .037) but cueing overall did not (30 % vs 22 %, p > .6); post-hoc "targeted dreamers" (12) rose from 20 % to 40 % (interaction χ²(1) = 4.18, p = .04); 4-min morning retest with yes/no feedback plus ~10-day Zoom follow-up; limitations: no no-cue control night, participants not fully blinded, post-hoc responder analysis — [OUP niaf067](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2026/1/niaf067/8456489)
- Pereira et al. (2023) — REM cueing improved SVRT rule-abstraction only at 1-week retest (d = 0.646); see KQ1 — [PMC10218979](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10218979/)
- Horowitz, Esfahany, Gálvez, Maes & Stickgold (2023), Scientific Reports 13:7319 (not TMR but closely related "targeted dream incubation" at N1): 49 analyzed in a 2 × 2 design (sleep/wake × incubation/none, n = 12–13 per cell); Dormio glove detected N1 from HR, EDA and finger-flexor changes and prompted "think of a tree"; 70.3 % of sleep-incubation reports contained the theme vs 1.4 % without incubation; creativity index SI 0.79 vs SN −0.07 vs WI −0.32 vs WN −0.47 (state p = .0002, incubation p = .015, interaction p = .049); theme incorporation predicted creativity (β = 0.26, p < .001); no EEG confirmation of N1 — [PMC10185495](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10185495/)

### Inferences
- The field's own newest data (Konkoly 2026) show that the effect of puzzle cueing may run through dream incorporation rather than cueing per se; a system that logs cue times and collects immediate dream reports (with blind incorporation coding) can test this mediator — something commercial apps cannot do.
- Sanders 2019's protocol details (unique 15-s soundtrack per puzzle, 2-min evening attempts, 3 cued / 3 uncued per night, 4-min morning retest, sound–puzzle recall test as a manipulation check) are a ready template; the retest should be blind to cue condition (Sanders used a double-blind algorithmic assignment).
- Beijamini 2021's null suggests choosing puzzle sets with many independent items (as Sanders/Konkoly did) rather than one all-or-none problem, because all-or-none insight tasks have low statistical sensitivity.

### Gaps
- No direct independent replication of Sanders 2019 with the same puzzle paradigm was found; the Konkoly 2026 study used REM (not SWS) and lucid dreamers, so it is a conceptual extension, not a replication.
- Effect sizes for Konkoly 2026 are reported as regression coefficients/odds ratios with an internal inconsistency (OR = .32 vs the stated direction) that should be checked in the supplement.

## KQ3. TLR and lucid-dream induction with sensory cues (lab, home, wearable, two-way communication, commercial claims)

### Takeaway
TLR (pre-sleep cue–mindset training + REM cueing) produced signal-verified lucid dreams in 50 % of participants in a lab nap (Carr 2020) and supports real-time two-way communication (Konkoly 2021; 18.4 % correct answers across 158 trials). Translation to home without EEG works modestly (Konkoly 2024: ~0.7 → ~2.1 lucid dreams/week, with frequent cue-induced awakenings), but the first preregistered multi-center PSG study (N = 60, 2024 preprint) found SVLD rates not different between REM cueing (38.3 %) and sham (28.3 %), with cues disrupting sleep in 23.7 % of cued periods — so induction gains over sham remain unproven, while cues reliably enter dreams and lengthen verified lucidity.

### Cited Findings
- Carr, Konkoly, Mallett, Edwards, Appel & Blagrove (2020; issue Dec 2023), Psychology of Consciousness: Theory, Research, and Practice 10(4), DOI 10.1037/cns0000227 (note: not Consciousness & Cognition): pre-sleep cue–lucidity training then cue re-presentation in REM during a morning nap; signal-verified lucid dreams in 50 % of cued participants vs 17 % of no-cue controls; three successful participants had never had a lucid dream; a later review cautions that cues may act partly via arousal/fragmentation — [Swansea Cronfa record](https://cronfa.swansea.ac.uk/Record/cronfa53997); [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/cns0000227)
- Konkoly et al. (2021), Current Biology (4 labs: Northwestern, Sorbonne/ICM, Osnabrück, Radboud): 36 participants in PSG-verified REM; 158 two-way-communication trials during signal-verified lucid REM: 29 correct (18.4 %), 5 incorrect (3.2 %), 28 ambiguous (17.7 %), 96 no response (60.8 %); correct answers came from 6 participants; 80.4 % of trials scored REM by all three experts; control: 379 attempts in non-lucid REM yielded 1 correct, 1 incorrect, 11 ambiguous, 366 none (Fisher p < .001); lucidity signaled by LRLR(LR) eye movements; answers via eye movements or zygomatic/corrugator EMG; Northwestern used auditory lucidity cues (one hypnogram shows the cue presented twice, a micro-arousal, then LRLRLR signals six times) — [Konkoly 2021 PDF](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northwestern.edu/dist/4/2492/files/2026/04/Konkoly_2021_Curr_Biol.pdf); [HAL record](https://hal.sorbonne-universite.fr/hal-03154112)
- Konkoly, Whitmore, Mallett, Mazurek & Paller (2024), Consciousness and Cognition 125:103759 — smartphone TLR without PSG. Exp 1 (Android, 19 completers of 26): lucid dreams/week 0.74 → 2.11 (Wilcoxon p = .007); 14/19 vs 8/19 had ≥1 LD; 14 of 38 LDs attributed to cues; cues = 400/600/800 Hz beeps (~650 ms) or 1-s violin; intensity started at discrimination threshold rising 0.16 % per presentation; cueing began ~6 h after training, every 20–40 s; accelerometer movement paused cues 5 min (Exp 1) / 1 min (Exp 2); cue-caused awakening paused 45 min. Exp 2 (public app, 416 users, 120 analyzable, 50 completed 7 nights): TLR vs combined controls × odd/even night interaction F(1,118) = 6.28, p = .01; untrained-cue nights had fewer lucid nights than TLR nights (t = −2.25, p = .03); no-cue nights did not differ (p = .77); night-2 TLR vs controls OR = 3.61, p = .006; all 236 users: no effect (p > .3); harp melodies (~3 s) with fades, intensity capped at discrimination threshold and at the level where the sleeper reported incorporating the sound; movement events per cue 0.19 vs 0.10 in silence (p = .002); the app "often woke participants, sometimes like an alarm clock"; LuCiD insight subscale (Voss 2013), LUSK, FILD used — [PMC11542932](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11542932)
- Mallett & Mallela (2024), International Journal of Dream Research 17(2):143–147 (also PsyArXiv): open-source iOS/watchOS app covering the whole TLR procedure (pre-sleep training, real-time sleep staging from Apple-Watch-derived measures, REM cueing); described as a "technical framework", no induction data — [IJoDR 10.11588/ijodr.2024.2.100797](https://doi.org/10.11588/ijodr.2024.2.100797); [PsyArXiv](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/e9u8v)
- Picard-Deland, Jafarzadeh Esfahani, Salvesen, …, Carr & Dresler (2024, bioRxiv 10.1101/2024.06.21.600133; current title "Inducing lucid dreaming with multisensory stimulation: a preregistered multi-center study"; PP): 60 participants (26.5 ± 6.2 y), Netherlands/Italy/Canada, two lab morning naps each, SSILD pre-sleep training paired with visual, auditory and tactile cues, cues re-presented in REM in one nap (sham in the other, counterbalanced), 2-channel EEG and the open-source Dreamento toolbox; SVLD in 31 participants (51.7 %); subjective lucidity in 52.5 % of naps, SVLD in 33.3 % of naps with no difference between stimulation (38.3 %) and sham (28.3 %); SVLD duration and number of pre-defined eye signals higher with cueing; cues perceived in 71.1 % of cued REM periods, acted as lucidity triggers in 39.1 % of stim SVLDs, and disrupted sleep in 23.7 % of cued periods — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.21.600133)
- Wolk, Morris, Nahas, Konkoly, Carr, Paller & Mallett (2023, PsyArXiv; PP): breath-counting (mindfulness) reactivation in REM after a ~5-h WBTB, ambient-music cues, N = 5, SVLD in 2 participants — [PsyArXiv 74kfg](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/74kfg)
- Erlacher, Furrer, Ineichen, Braillard & Schmid (2022), Clocks & Sleep: WBTB + MILD originally induced LDs in 50 % of unselected participants in one lab night; shortening uninterrupted sleep to 4.5 h gave 26 % (4/15), time-based (non-REM) awakening gave 0 % (0/20), reality testing instead of MILD gave 20 % (3/15); "without REM awakenings in the morning, lucid dream induction failed" — [PMC9149976](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9149976/)
- Gott et al. (2020), Consciousness and Cognition: multi-centre data — self-assessed awakenings, polyphasic sleep and physiologically validated wake→REM transitions were associated with lucid dreaming, but validated awakening counts and sleep quality were not — [doi:10.1016/j.concog.2020.102988](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2020.102988)
- Tan & Fan (2023), Journal of Sleep Research 32:e13786 (systematic review 2010–2022): 19 peer-reviewed studies, 14 techniques; MILD most effective; SSILD and galantamine "competitive candidates" needing replication; WBTB treated as an add-on condition; 17 studies moderate, 3 poor quality — [doi:10.1111/jsr.13786](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13786)
- Aspy (2020), International Lucid Dream Induction Study (baseline): MILD and SSILD similarly effective; hybrid no better — [PMC7379166](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7379166/)
- Schredl (2025), IJoDR four-week diary study: neither reality testing nor autosuggestion significantly increased LD frequency; differences were explained by prior LD frequency (details from catalogue record/search summary only) — [HEIDI record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1938010094)
- Mota-Rolim et al. (2019), Frontiers in Neuroscience "Portable devices to induce lucid dreams — are they reliable?" (baseline review of 10 commercial devices incl. ZMax) — [PMC6517539](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6517539/)
- Lucid dreaming and sleep quality (2023, Psychology of Consciousness, online N = 489): LD frequency not associated with poorer overall sleep quality or dissociation; associated with more sleep disturbances but better subjective sleep quality; ~10 % of LDs emotionally negative; correlational — [NYU abstract page](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2023/journal-article-abstracts/cns0000288/); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/can-lucid-dreaming-be-dangerous-new-research-suggests-lucid-dreaming-is-generally-a-safe-and-positive-experience/)
- REMspace (COMM / partly PR): Raduga (2022), Sleep Science 15(2):149–157 — four LD practitioners produced the EMG signature of "I love you" in lab-verified lucid dreams — [Thieme PDF](https://thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.5935/1984-0063.20220035.pdf); a Remmyo proof-of-concept (IJoDR, accepted Dec 2025, online Jan 2026) translated facial EMG into sounds among 7 experienced lucid dreamers who induced 18 LDs over two nights — [IJoDR file](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/IJoDR/libraryFiles/downloadPublic/951); the Oct 2024 "first dream-to-dream message" claim rests on a company press release, not a peer-reviewed paper — [Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/videos/dream-communication); [Anadolu Agency](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/california-startup-tests-communication-between-people-in-dreams/3757116)
- Prophetic "Halo"/Morpheus-1 (COMM): transcranial focused ultrasound + EEG headband announced Jan 2024; no peer-reviewed outcome data found; experts quoted as skeptical — [Live Science](https://livescience.com/technology/virtual-reality/ultrasonic-headband-induces-lucid-dreams-using-ai-its-creators-claim-but-an-expert-remains-skeptical)

### Inferences
- The strongest TLR evidence is lab-based with PSG-verified REM and eye-signal verification; home TLR without EEG relies on self-report and timers and fragments sleep. An open-source system with real-time frontal-EEG REM gating sits exactly in the gap the Konkoly 2024 authors flag ("REM-only triggering" as the needed improvement).
- The 2024 preregistered multi-center null for induction-rate vs sham means any home system should treat "lucid dreams per week vs sham-cue nights" as the primary outcome and pre-register it, with cue-incorporation and LD duration as secondary outcomes.
- Cue types with evidence: pure tones/beeps, violin/harp melodies, ambient music (auditory); visual + tactile added in the Dresler protocol; verbal phrases during training only. No evidence favors one cue type; Konkoly 2024's violin-vs-beep comparison was unpowered.
- Expectation/demand effects are a live confound (Konkoly 2024, 2026 both list it); blinded sham-cue nights (untrained sound) are the control the literature now uses.

### Gaps
- Carr 2020 sample size and exact cue parameters were not retrievable (repository returned 503/connection resets); figures above come from the published abstract.
- No peer-reviewed home TLR study with wearable EEG (Dreem/Zmax/Muse) was found; the Dresler multi-center study is lab-based and still a preprint (a Sleep Medicine 2024 abstract was referenced in search results but could not be verified).
- Validation accuracy of the Mallett & Mallela watchOS REM detector was not available in the abstract.

## KQ4. Safety and arousal: cue volume, cue-induced arousals, closed-loop volume adaptation, EEG arousal markers

### Takeaway
Across 2019–2025 studies, cues that provoke even brief EEG arousals weaken the reactivated memories (Whitmore & Paller 2023; Whitmore 2022 ×2; Göldi & Rasch 2019), and home cueing measurably increases movement/awakening (Konkoly 2024; Dresler 2024: sleep disruption in 23.7 % of cued REM periods). Reported safe practice: start near the perceptual threshold (~20–30 dB SPL above or within a 40–45 dB noise bed), titrate up only while EEG shows no arousal, pause 30–90 s on movement/alpha/EMG, stricter thresholds in REM, and log every cue and arousal.

### Cited Findings
- Whitmore & Paller (2023), Learning & Memory 30:63: N = 24; loud cues ≈43 dBA tuned to produce brief arousals, soft cues ≈28 dBA (33 % of initial arousal threshold) tuned to avoid them; arousals (AASM, within 10 s) followed 59 % of loud and 43 % of soft cues; cued objects were forgotten more than uncued (t(23) = 2.39, p = .025); cued-with-arousal objects were forgotten more than uncued (t(22) = 3.28, p = .003); cued-without-arousal not significantly different; pre-cue 10–12.5 Hz power predicted forgetting (uncorrected); authors conclude "the presence of arousal may matter more than absolute volume" — [Learn Mem](https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/30/3/63); [bioRxiv full text](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.04.10.487316v1.full)
- Whitmore, Bassard & Paller (2022), npj Science of Learning: nap TMR of face–name pairs; benefit correlated positively with N3 duration and negatively with cue-evoked sleep disruption indexed by increased alpha-band EEG in the 5 s after a cue — [PMC8755782](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8755782/)
- Whitmore, Harris, Kovach & Paller (2022), Journal of Sleep Research 31:e13731 — "SleepStim" home TMR with Fitbit + phone (+ Dreem 2 for validation): neural-net p(N3) from accelerometer/gyro/HR; cues only when p(N3) ≥ 0.9 over 240 s and latest ≥ 0.85; 10-s onset-to-onset intervals; up to 30 min of cueing per night; ~67 cues per cued night; intensity set-point decreased automatically if p(N3) dropped below 0.85 while sounds played (cue-evoked arousal); Exp 1 (N = 61): no overall TMR effect but benefit correlated with maximum cue intensity — high-intensity settings "almost always showed a reversed TMR effect"; Exp 2 (N = 24, intensity capped ≤0.02 AU, ≥25 cues on the adaptation night): significant TMR benefit (z = 2.69, p = .007); 13 % of participants reported hearing any cue; 16 % of cues fell in wake per Dreem staging; recommendation: strictly limit cue intensity and adjust during the night; code at github.com/nathanww/home-tmr — [doi:10.1111/jsr.13731](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13731); [author PDF](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northwestern.edu/dist/4/2492/files/2026/04/Whitmore_2022_J_Sleep_Res.pdf)
- Göldi & Rasch (2019): unsupervised home TMR impaired memory in participants reporting word-specific disturbance; authors call automatic sleep monitoring and avoidance of auditory-induced awakenings "a precondition" for home TMR — [PMC6497651](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6497651/)
- Konkoly et al. (2024): movement events per cue 0.19 after TLR cues vs 0.10 in silent periods (W = 59, p = .002); app frequently woke users; unknown home noise and stage-dependent arousal thresholds made intensity hard to set — [PMC11542932](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11542932)
- Picard-Deland/Dresler (2024, PP): multisensory REM cues disrupted sleep in 23.7 % of cued REM periods — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.21.600133)
- Hutchison et al. (2021): cues embedded in ~40 dB SPL brown noise; playback paused on movement, stage change or visible alpha — [PMC7994443](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7994443/)
- Ngo & Staresina (2022): titration from 20 dB SPL in 6-dB steps until a clear EEG response without arousal — [PMC9636934](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9636934)
- Salfi et al. (2025), Journal of Sleep Research 34:e70000 (home, Dreem 2): pseudoword cues at 45 dB via built-in speakers preceded by a 100-ms pink-noise bone-conduction trigger; stimulation after 5 min stable N3, ≥9 s between stimuli, paused 30 s on movement or alpha; ~167 ± 62 cues per night; one participant withdrew because the headband made sleep difficult — [PMC12592824](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12592824/)
- Borghese et al. (2022), Frontiers in Psychiatry (home REM TMR, Dreem): cue every 10 s after >5 min REM; pauses after movement (90 s), alpha (45 s), blink (10 s), poor signal (4 s), resume after 5 min REM; volume chosen by participant in app; TMR participants were marginally less vigilant (p = .08) and total sleep/N2 fell during the home week — [PMC9281560](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9281560/)
- Lustenberger et al. (2022), Communications Medicine (home CLAS, older adults): volume started at 52 dB, +1.5 dB after seven arousal-free tones up to 60 dB, −1.5 dB after arousals on two consecutive stimuli down to 46 dB; stimulation blocked in down-phase, within 0.5 s of a prior stimulus, or when EEG > 300 µV; REM showed a trend to reduction (p = .059/.075) and morning mood was lower on stimulation nights (p = .021/.011), correlating with REM reduction — [PMC9053232](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9053232)
- Konkoly et al. (2026): cues from the lowest detectable volume, increased only while soundly asleep, paused on EMG increase, alpha, or loss of REM eye movements/theta — [OUP niaf067](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2026/1/niaf067/8456489)
- Coon, Nilsson, Smith & Reid (2026), Frontiers in Neuroscience 19:1682450 (design review for closed-loop sleep neurostimulation): calibrate per subject near perceptual threshold and titrate to the highest non-arousing volume with hard caps; ≤1–2 pulses per SO up-state, stop after 3–5 consecutive detections with a 10–20 s quiet window; duty-cycle 3–5 min on/off; "use stricter settings in REM" because arousal thresholds are lower than in N3; screen for sleep-disordered breathing, insomnia, tinnitus; devices "should log stimuli/arousals, encrypt data, make algorithms and safety interlocks transparent" with user pause/stop; forehead EEG is artifact-prone, so use PPG/accelerometry to flag artifacts; end-to-end latency < 100–150 ms for phase targeting — [PMC12914948](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914948/)
- Beijamini et al. (2021): SWS-cued participants showed more wake time and less N1 than undisturbed sleepers (exploratory) — [PMC7965947](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7965947/)

### Inferences
- A REM-gated system's arousal guard should treat alpha bursts (~5 s post-cue window; Whitmore 2022 npj), EMG rise, movement, loss of REM theta/eye movements and HR jumps as block/pause reasons, with 30–90 s pauses and a step-down in volume — this mirrors every protocol above and is the main lever separating benefit from harm.
- Report "arousal-per-cue" rate as a primary safety metric (benchmarks: 43–59 % per cue in the deliberately disruptive Whitmore 2023 lab study; 0.19 vs 0.10 movement events per cue in Konkoly 2024; 23.7 % of cued REM periods disrupted in Dresler 2024).
- Volume should be expressed relative to an in-situ noise bed and the user's own detection threshold rather than absolute dB, because home noise is unknown (Konkoly 2024) and absolute levels in the literature range from ~28 dBA cues to 46–60 dB CLAS tones.

### Gaps
- No study quantifies a dose–response between cue count per REM period and next-day sleepiness or REM fragmentation; recommended cooldowns (e.g., 45-min pause after a cue-caused awakening in Konkoly 2024; 10–20 s quiet windows in Coon 2026) are heuristics, not tested parameters.
- K-complex-based arousal metrics for TMR cues were not found in the retrieved REM-cueing papers (K-complexes are an NREM phenomenon; an NREM study found unfamiliar voices evoked more K-complexes/micro-arousals — [PMC8896625](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8896625/)).

## KQ5. Physiological targeting refinements: phasic vs tonic REM, REM eye-movement bursts, SO up-state CLAS, spindle timing, theta, frontal-only EEG

### Takeaway
Tonic REM is the more "externally connected" microstate (higher arousability, stronger evoked responses), and informative sounds are actively suppressed during eye-movement bursts (Koroma 2020), so cueing between eye-movement bursts is the mechanistically favored window — but no study has yet compared TMR/TLR outcomes in tonic vs phasic REM. In NREM, up-state-locked closed-loop cueing is now replicated (Ngo & Staresina 2022; Nicolas 2025) and runs on single frontal channels and consumer headbands; forehead/single-channel staging reaches only κ ≈ 0.37–0.45 vs PSG in home data, which bounds what frontal-only devices can resolve.

### Cited Findings
- Simor, van der Wijk, Nobili & Peigneux (2020), Sleep Medicine Reviews 52:101305: phasic and tonic REM "are remarkably different neural states with respect to environmental alertness, spontaneous and evoked cortical activity, information processing" — [doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2020.101305](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2020.101305)
- Koroma, Lacaux, Andrillon, Legendre, Léger & Kouider (2020), Current Biology 30:2411–2417: EEG speech reconstruction shows informative speech is amplified in REM outside eye movements and selectively suppressed during rapid eye movements — [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2020.04.047](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2020.04.047)
- Salzburg high-density EEG study (17 sleepers; preprint/abstract, authors not verified): tonic REM showed more micro-arousals and stronger event-related responses to voices/names than phasic REM; eye movements reacted faster to unfamiliar voices and own name even in phasic episodes — [Salzburg Pure record](https://uni-salzburg.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/preferential-neural-and-ocular-responses-to-external-stimuli-duri/)
- Borghese et al. (2022) list as a limitation that cues were given "regardless of REM tonic or phasic state, though phasic REM may matter more for extinction" — [PMC9281560](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9281560/)
- Pereira et al. (2023): REM cueing was continuous in only 1 of 26 participants because of arousal/stage-change interruptions, vs 19 of 26 for SWS — [PMC10218979](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10218979/)
- Abdellahi et al. (2023), eLife: REM reactivation was detectable ~1 s post-cue; theta increased after cues on both experimental and adaptation nights (sound processing, not memory) and theta-only classification was not above chance — [eLife 84324](https://elifesciences.org/articles/84324)
- Abdellahi, Koopman, Treder & Lewis (2021, bioRxiv; PP): in SWS, cues on the up-going SO transition were more likely to yield classifiable reactivation (p = .019); pre-cue SO features and fewer pre-cue spindles predicted reactivation; authors propose using pre-cue SO/spindle features "to deliver cues only when they are most likely to be effective", reducing cue count and arousals, and explicitly for "wearable, home-based TMR devices" — [bioRxiv 471945](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.12.09.471945v1)
- Ngo & Staresina (2022): single-channel Fz closed loop (OpenViBE) with amplitude thresholds and an 8-s refractory period reproduced up-state benefits — [PMC9636934](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9636934)
- Nicolas et al. (2025), Nature Communications: up- vs down-state TMR for motor memory; up-state cueing improved performance and SO amplitude/sigma — [PMC11920436](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11920436/)
- Salfi et al. (2025): consumer Dreem 2 (5 dry electrodes, F7–O1/F8–O2 analyzed) with a fixed 750-ms delay after built-in slow-wave detection landed cues "on average in the ascending phase" (not phase-locked), yet cued pseudowords improved +8.6 % vs −4.6 % uncued (OR 1.78 [1.17–2.71], p = .007); spindle-band power at the second ERP peak predicted recall — [PMC12592824](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12592824/)
- Lustenberger et al. (2022): one frontal channel (Fpz–A2) with a phase-locked loop targeting ~45° of the slow-wave up-phase, delta/beta criteria, 50-ms pink-noise bursts; robust low-SWA (0.75–1.25 Hz) increase (windowed F(1,12.7) = 18.1, p ≈ .001; continuous F = 9.01, p = .011); strong responders were younger (d = −2.74) and baseline lowSWA predicted responder nights with ~91 % accuracy — [PMC9053232](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9053232)
- Debellemaniere et al. (2018), Frontiers in Human Neuroscience (home dry-EEG CLAS baseline): real-time N3 detection sensitivity 0.70, specificity 0.90 vs PSG — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00088/text)
- Bressler, Neely, Yost & Wang (2024), Scientific Reports (Elemind, company-sponsored RCT, N = 21 analyzed): acoustic pulses anti-phase to alpha for 30 min at bedtime reduced EEG-scored sleep-onset latency by 10.5 ± 15.9 min (29.3 ± 44.4 %), p = .0019 — [PMC11156862](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11156862/)
- Parry & Briganti (2026, medRxiv; PP), Wearanize+ dataset (100 home recordings, Zmax F7–Fpz/F8–Fpz vs PSG): 10 % of recordings failed from dropout and 16 % were degraded; in adequate recordings YASA κ = 0.450 vs Dreamento κ = 0.371; both confuse N2/N3; "channel topology is the primary limiting factor for frontal single-channel" staging — [medRxiv](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.01.26354591)
- Coon et al. (2026): fixed 300–500 ms delays after the detected negative peak are "common and effective"; end-to-end latency < 100–150 ms; tolerant phase window in young adults; open question whether < 10 ms precision matters — [PMC12914948](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914948/)
- Hutchison et al. (2021) and Yuksel et al. (2025) show REM theta/delta and spindle modulation by cue valence but no REM-phase targeting — [PMC7994443](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7994443/); [PMC11930935](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11930935/)

### Inferences
- A frontal-EEG wearable can implement two evidence-aligned refinements today: (1) a tonic-REM sub-gate (cue only when no eye-movement burst in the last few seconds, using EOG-like frontal deflections), and (2) phase/delay-locked NREM cueing with a fixed 300–500 ms delay after a detected down-state, which recent reviews consider adequate. Both are untested as *outcome* moderators in REM-TLR/TMR, so they are research features, not proven enhancements.
- Frontal-only staging κ ≈ 0.4 means REM gating must rely on conjunctions (theta without alpha, low EMG proxy, eye-movement signatures, HR variability) and on stability windows (≥5 min REM in Borghese 2022/Salfi 2025 analogues) rather than single epochs — supporting a "stable gate" design.

### Gaps
- No study was found that cues in tonic vs phasic REM and compares memory or lucidity outcomes; the Salzburg tonic/phasic study's authorship and peer-review status could not be verified.
- No home wearable CLAS/TMR study used a Muse device; Dreem (discontinued), Zmax and Fitbit+phone are the validated platforms.

## KQ6. Protocol and methodology standards (controls, blinding, outcome measures, dream coding, reporting)

### Takeaway
No TMR- or TLR-specific reporting checklist exists; the field's de-facto standards are within-subject cued vs uncued items, blinded untrained-sound (sham) nights, algorithmic double-blind assignment, manipulation checks (cue–item recall), AASM-scored arousals per cue, blind coding of dream reports for cue and conceptual incorporation, and — increasingly — preregistration and multi-center designs, with repeated calls for higher power.

### Cited Findings
- Hu et al. (2020): within- and between-subject TMR designs are equally effective; most individual effect sizes are non-significant, so authors recommend high-powered designs informed by meta-analytic estimates — [Hu et al. 2020 PDF](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/paller/PsychBulletin2020.pdf)
- Carbone & Diekelmann (2024): recommend larger samples, multi-lab studies of established paradigms and preregistration; warn that home TMR arousals "may impair or even weaken memories" — [PMC11018807](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018807/)
- Cordi & Rasch (2021): replication failures and boundary conditions; call for "higher standards for reproducible science" in sleep–memory research — [doi:10.1016/j.conb.2020.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conb.2020.06.002)
- Sanders et al. (2019): cue assignment by algorithm so experimenter and participant were double-blind; sound–puzzle recall test as manipulation check (71.9 %, no cued/uncued difference); not preregistered — [PMC6843748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6843748)
- Konkoly et al. (2024): control conditions = untrained novel sound vs no sound on alternating nights, participants blind; outcomes = LuCiD insight subscale (Voss 2013), LUSK, FILD, "did you wake because of a sound?" item; limitations listed: self-report only, convenience sample with high prior LD, attrition, expectation effects, unpowered cue-type comparison — [PMC11542932](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11542932)
- Konkoly et al. (2026): two blind raters coded dream reports for sound-cue incorporation, conceptual incorporation (direct/indirect), lucidity and solving attempts; real-time sniff signals within 15 s of a cue and LRLR lucidity signals; limitations: not fully blinded participants, demand characteristics, no no-cue night, post-hoc responder analysis — [OUP niaf067](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2026/1/niaf067/8456489)
- Picard-Deland/Dresler (2024): preregistered, multi-center, counterbalanced stimulation vs sham naps, signal-verified lucidity and cue-perception verified by intentional eye movements — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.21.600133)
- Whitmore & Paller (2023): AASM arousal scoring in a 10-s post-cue window by a rater blind to cue type; convenience sample, not preregistered — [Learn Mem](https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/30/3/63)
- Konkoly et al. (2021): responses rated by three/four raters blind to condition; trials categorized correct/incorrect/ambiguous/no-response; sham/control trials in non-lucid REM to estimate false positives — [Konkoly 2021 PDF](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northwestern.edu/dist/4/2492/files/2026/04/Konkoly_2021_Curr_Biol.pdf)
- eLife public review of Abdellahi et al. (2023): reviewers demanded clarity on classifier features and trial-exclusion justification and removal of under-supported claims — an example of what reviewers expect in TMR methods — [eLife peer reviews](https://elifesciences.org/articles/84324/peer-reviews)
- Lustenberger et al. (2022): participants and assessors blinded, randomized block order, two-week washout, Sham nights recorded triggers without sound — [PMC9053232](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9053232)
- Schwartz et al. (2022) used nightmares/week at 2 weeks as a pre-specified primary outcome with 3-month follow-up — [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032)

### Inferences
- A minimal "TMR/TLR protocol fidelity" record consistent with the literature: per-cue timestamp, stage/gate state, volume, arousal flag within 10 s, pause reason; per-night cue counts per condition; per-item cue condition hidden from the morning tester; manipulation check (cue–item recall); dream reports collected before any condition is revealed and coded by a blind rater; preregistered primary outcome with sham-cue nights.
- Nothing like a TMR CONSORT extension exists; adopting SCRIBE/CENT reporting items (KQ7) is the closest formal standard a self-experiment can cite.

### Gaps
- No published "TMR reporting checklist" or consensus statement was found in 2021–2026 searches.
- Hall–Van de Castle coding was not used in any retrieved TMR/TLR study; LuCiD (Voss 2013) is the lucidity scale actually used.

## KQ7. N-of-1 / self-experimentation methodology relevant to a home sleep self-experiment

### Takeaway
N-of-1 trials are randomized multi-crossover single-participant experiments; recent reviews (Schmid & Yang 2022; Konigorski, Ried-Larsen & Schmid 2026) and open tools (StudyU) cover design, Bayesian analysis with carry-over/trend/autocorrelation, and aggregation across individuals, while CENT 2015 and SCRIBE 2016 give reporting checklists.

### Cited Findings
- Schmid & Yang (2022), Harvard Data Science Review: N-of-1 trials are "a type of single-case experimental design in which two or more interventions are applied multiple times in a randomized order to an individual participant"; Bayesian models can incorporate external information, yield posterior probabilities for the individual, and extend to trend, carry-over and autocorrelation; also cover series of N-of-1 trials — [HDSR](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/b6efwlql)
- Konigorski, Ried-Larsen & Schmid (2026), Experimental Physiology (arXiv 2412.15076): introduces key design components, analysis/interpretation and digital tools for N-of-1 trials; results "can be aggregated across multiple study participants to provide population-level inferences more efficiently than standard group randomized trials"; warns of carry-over effects and time trends — [doi:10.1113/EP092753](https://doi.org/10.1113/EP092753); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15076)
- StudyU (2022), JMIR: open-source platform (designer + smartphone app) for designing and running digital N-of-1 trials — [JMIR](https://jmir.org/2022/7/e35884)
- SCRIBE 2016 (Tate et al.), 26-item reporting checklist for single-case experimental designs in behavioural interventions; CENT 2015 (Vohra/Shamseer) is the CONSORT extension for medical N-of-1 trials — [PMC4960517](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4960517/)
- Bayesian/adaptive methods "hold promise for increasing the informational yield of single-patient trials while reducing participant burden, but are not widely used" (systematic review of N-of-1 trials to 2015) — [PMC6128372](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6128372)
- Within-sleep-research precedents for crossover/sham designs usable as N-of-1 blocks: Lustenberger 2022 (2-week stim / 2-week sham with washout, blinded) and Konkoly 2024 Exp 2 (alternating TLR vs sham nights) — [PMC9053232](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9053232); [PMC11542932](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11542932)

### Inferences
- A home TMR/TLR self-experiment maps naturally onto an N-of-1 design: randomized blocks of cue nights vs sham-cue nights (untrained sound at the same volume), with washout nights, pre-specified outcomes (LD/week, cued vs uncued solve rate, arousals per cue), and a Bayesian model that accounts for night-to-night autocorrelation and first-night/habituation effects (Göldi & Rasch 2019 found benefit only on night 3).
- Reporting under SCRIBE/CENT items (randomization, blinding, sequence, adherence, data loss) would make single-user results citable.

### Gaps
- No published N-of-1 study of TMR or TLR was found; sample-size/power heuristics for N-of-1 sleep cueing (number of nights per arm) are not available in the retrieved literature.

## KQ8. Emerging directions 2024–2026 (clinical TMR, home closed-loop TMR, olfactory, language, dream engineering, personalized cues, open toolboxes)

### Takeaway
The most consequential recent work is clinical and home-based: REM-TMR added to imagery rehearsal therapy reduced nightmares (N = 36, Dreem at home), SO-phase-locked TMR after EMDR in PTSD (n = 17 vs 16) increased SO/spindles and reduced avoidance without disturbing sleep, and closed-loop vocabulary TMR with a consumer headband works at home. Dream engineering now spans N1 incubation (Dormio), REM puzzle cueing with lucid dreamers, and open toolboxes (Dreamento); LLM-generated cues have no literature yet.

### Cited Findings
- Schwartz, Clerget & Perogamvros (2022), Current Biology 32:4808–4816: 36 nightmare-disorder patients; TMR group heard a piano chord while imagining a positive ending during IRT, then for 2 weeks at home a Dreem headband delivered the chord during REM; fewer nightmares and more positive dream emotions than IRT-only at 2 weeks, sustained at 3 months; press figures: ~3/week → <0.5/week (TMR) vs 1.5/week (control) at 3 months — [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032); [UNIGE record](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:165230); [Technology Networks summary](https://www.technologynetworks.com/neuroscience/news/researchers-reduce-nightmare-frequency-by-altering-dreamers-emotions-367019)
- van der Heijden, van der Werf, van den Heuvel, Talamini & van Marle (2024), Current Biology: PTSD patients after one EMDR session received SO-phase-targeted TMR with EMDR clicks (modeling-based closed-loop, n = 17) or sham (n = 16); TMR increased SO and spindle dynamics, correlating with symptom reduction; larger reduction of avoidance during script-driven imagery; "TMR did not disturb sleep or trigger nightmares"; "first proof of principle" — [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2024.07.019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2024.07.019)
- Borghese et al. (2022), Frontiers in Psychiatry: 48 social-anxiety patients, VR exposure + home REM TMR (Dreem) for ~1 week; no group difference on subjective distress (group × time p = .986) or skin conductance; RMSSD correlated with weekly REM-cue count only in the TMR group (τ = 0.36, p = .019) — [PMC9281560](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9281560/)
- Salfi et al. (2025), Journal of Sleep Research: first home closed-loop TMR for vocabulary with a consumer EEG headband (Dreem 2); cued +8.6 % vs uncued −4.6 %, OR 1.78, p = .007; 7 of 31 excluded for technical/signal problems; the stimulation platform was discontinued by the manufacturer — [PMC12592824](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12592824/)
- Olfactory home TMR: Amores, Mehra, Rasch & Maes (CHI 2023) smartphone-controlled scent wearable, 32 participants, odors released during tasks and replayed at home during sleep; recall gains strongest for spatial tasks — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/memory/); Carbone & Diekelmann 2024 also cite school-based odor TMR at home (Neumann 2020; Vidal 2022) and the Ezzence olfactometer as untested for TMR — [PMC11018807](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018807/)
- Horowitz et al. (2023), Scientific Reports: N1 targeted dream incubation raised creativity and 70.3 % theme incorporation (see KQ2) — [PMC10185495](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10185495/)
- Konkoly et al. (2026): "dream engineering" of unsolved puzzles in REM with TLR — [OUP niaf067](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2026/1/niaf067/8456489)
- Carr, Haar, Amores, Lopes, Bernal, Vega, Rosello, Jain & Maes (2020), Consciousness and Cognition 83:102955 "Dream engineering: Simulating worlds through sensory stimulation": argues nearly any sensory stimulus (haptic, temperature, vestibular, olfactory, auditory, borrowed from VR hardware) can modulate sleep experience; discusses ethics of targeted dream direction — [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/dream-engineering-simulating-worlds-through-sensory-stimulation/)
- Coon et al. (2026): "design notes for the next generation of closed-loop neurostimulation technology" — hardware abstraction layers, auxiliary PPG/accelerometer artifact flags, transparent interlocks, logging — [PMC12914948](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914948/)
- Open tooling: Dreamento (open-source dream-engineering toolbox for Zmax, used in the Dresler multi-center TLR study) — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.21.600133); Dreamento vs YASA home-staging comparison (κ 0.37 vs 0.45; Dreamento more robust on degraded signals) — [medRxiv](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.01.26354591); SleepStim code (Whitmore 2022) at github.com/nathanww/home-tmr — [doi:10.1111/jsr.13731](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13731); Mallett & Mallela open-source watchOS TLR app — [IJoDR](https://doi.org/10.11588/ijodr.2024.2.100797)
- Personalized/adaptive cueing (PP, arXiv Nov 2025, Korea University): cue frequency adapted to each person's retrieval performance and item difficulty in a word-pair task; authors report reduced decay and stronger SO–spindle synchronization vs fixed cueing — not peer-reviewed, no LLM involved — [arXiv 2511.15013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15013v1)
- Elemind (2024) alpha-phase-locked stimulation for sleep-onset insomnia — company-sponsored RCT, N = 21 — [PMC11156862](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11156862/)
- Greco et al. (2025) and Sato et al. (2025): REM-TMR of negative material modulates salience-network/LPP responses without memory change (see KQ1) — [PMC12516159](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12516159/); [PMC12199615](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12199615/)

### Inferences
- REM-specific TMR has its clearest translational foothold in emotional/nightmare work (Schwartz 2022), which used exactly the stack an open-source Muse system reproduces: home headband, automatic REM detection, one trained sound, 2-week protocol, nightmare diary. Replicating that design (with a sham sound) is a feasible, high-value validation target that avoids efficacy claims about lucidity.
- Because the two consumer platforms used in published home TMR (Dreem 2, Zmax) are discontinued or proprietary, an open-source Muse-based pipeline would fill a documented reproducibility gap (Salfi 2025 explicitly notes the Dreem stimulation system "was discontinued due to company policy changes").

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or preprint study of LLM-generated personalized TMR cues was found (search returned only adaptive-frequency cueing).
- No TMR-for-insomnia trial was found; insomnia appears only as an exclusion/safety criterion (Coon 2026).
- 2025–2026 TLR follow-ups beyond Konkoly 2026 and the Dresler preprint could not be checked after the search budget ran out.

## KQ9. What evidence-based features would make an open-source home REM-gated cueing system (REMind) scientifically distinctive?

### Takeaway
The literature converges on a short list the project already partly implements: EEG-verified stable REM gating with arousal-contingent pausing and volume step-down, within-subject cued/uncued randomization with blinded morning retests, per-cue structured logs (stage, volume, arousal), blind dream-report coding for cue/conceptual incorporation, delayed (1-week) retests, sham-cue nights, preregistered N-of-1 analysis, and a tonic-REM sub-gate as a labeled research option — none of which commercial lucid-dream devices publish.

### Cited Findings
- Non-EEG-supervised cueing had no pooled benefit (g = 0.26, ns) in Hu 2020, while N2/SWS cueing did — [Hu et al. 2020 PDF](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/paller/PsychBulletin2020.pdf)
- Konkoly 2024 name "REM-only triggering" as the needed improvement of home TLR; Carbone & Diekelmann 2024 call home arousals the main risk — [PMC11542932](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11542932); [PMC11018807](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018807/)
- Whitmore 2022 (SleepStim) automatically lowered cue intensity when p(N3) dropped during sounds and capped intensity in Exp 2, turning a null into a significant effect — [doi:10.1111/jsr.13731](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13731)
- Whitmore & Paller 2023: arousal within 10 s of a cue converts benefit into forgetting — [Learn Mem](https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/30/3/63)
- Pereira 2023 and Rakowska 2021: TMR effects emerged only at 1 week / 10 days — [PMC10218979](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10218979/); [PMC8591408](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8591408/)
- Konkoly 2026: dream incorporation, not cueing per se, predicted solving; blind coders used — [OUP niaf067](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2026/1/niaf067/8456489)
- Picard-Deland/Dresler 2024: preregistered sham-controlled design showed cues enter dreams (71.1 % of cued REM periods) and lengthen SVLDs even when induction rate vs sham was null — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.21.600133)
- Koroma 2020 and Simor 2020: sensory gating during eye-movement bursts; tonic REM more responsive — [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2020.04.047](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2020.04.047); [doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2020.101305](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2020.101305)
- Coon 2026: devices should log stimuli/arousals, expose algorithms and interlocks, allow user stop, and use stricter REM settings — [PMC12914948](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914948/)
- Schwartz 2022 shows a home REM-TMR therapeutic template with a pre-specified primary outcome — [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032)
- SCRIBE 2016 / CENT 2015 / Konigorski 2026 give reporting and analysis frameworks for single-user trials — [PMC4960517](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4960517/); [doi:10.1113/EP092753](https://doi.org/10.1113/EP092753)

### Inferences
- Distinctive, defensible claims for REMind would be about *protocol fidelity and measurability* (verified REM gating, arousal-per-cue rate, blinded randomization, structured logs, open code and data formats) rather than induction or learning efficacy, which the best-controlled studies still find modest or null.
- Concrete feature recommendations grounded above: (a) arousal guard with 5–10 s post-cue alpha/EMG/movement window and automatic volume step-down; (b) ≥5-min stable-REM requirement before first cue, 10–30 s inter-cue spacing, duty-cycled cueing and a 45-min pause after a cue-attributed awakening; (c) optional tonic-REM sub-gate; (d) sham-cue (untrained sound) nights scheduled by the randomizer; (e) 1-week delayed puzzle retest; (f) dream-report capture before unblinding with incorporation coding fields; (g) N-of-1 analysis export with SCRIBE/CENT items; (h) a nightmare/IRT-style use case replicating Schwartz 2022's design.

### Gaps
- Whether any of these features changes outcomes in a Muse-based system is untested; the recommendation is to position them as hypotheses to be evaluated with the project's own validation metrics, not as proven advantages.
